\begin{theorem}

	Suppose $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ injective, $a \in ^{*}\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{R}$. Then $f(a) \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{R}$.

\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}

	By the choice of the fol, we already have $f$ is a function symbol of our language. This guarantees that $f(a)$ makes sense.
	Let $r = f(a)$, that is $^{*} \mathbb{R} \models f(a) = r$. By contradiction, let's say $r \in \mathbb{R}$.
	Since $\mathbb{R} \models f \text{ injective }$, the same goes for $^{*} \mathbb{R}$.
	Since $^{*}\mathbb{R} \models \exists x f(x) = r$, by elementarity the same goes for $\mathbb{R}$.
	Let $d \in \mathbb{R}$ be such that $\mathbb{R} \models f(d) = r$.
	Then $^{*}\mathbb{R} \models f(d) = r \land f(a) = r$. This is a contradiction since $^{*}\mathbb{R} \models f \text{ injective }$ and 
	$a \not = d$ since $a \not \in \mathbb{R}$ while $d \in \mathbb{R}$.

\end{proof}
